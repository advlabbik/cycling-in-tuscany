No matter how carefully you plan a cycling trip from home, sitting at your computer, studying GPX tracks and cross-checking information about lodging and food, **once you leave the digital world for the road**—whether gravel or asphalt—**everything changes**. Covering just a few kilometers might take only a few hours, but there is one variable that technology cannot predict and that changes everything: *where* you ride.

In Tuscany, for example, it's hard to cycle even 50 km without detours and stops, because **there is something to see behind every bend.** It could be a breathtaking view at the top of a climb, a Romanesque church at the end of a descent, an archaeological excavation on a side road chosen at random, or perhaps a snack at an unexpected inn.

This is a region that combines the pleasure of effort with the spirit of adventure, yet without too many worries. There are countless routes that can be customized according to each rider's experience. Tuscany's ancient tradition of welcoming tourists, travelers, pilgrims, and wanderers of every kind has enriched it with endless options for sleeping, eating, and resting. That's what makes cycling here such a unique experience: **everyone can set their own pace, according to personal condition and fitness level.**

One example among many is the loop from San Quirico d'Orcia – Montalcino – Bagno Vignoni. Approximately 80 kilometres in one of the most popular areas for cyclists and bikepackers alike. Anyone who comes to ride here already knows, thanks to the many events held throughout the year: the Tuscany Trail (the most famous bikepacking event in the world), historic cycling races, granfondos, and gravel gatherings. Iconic events that weave through fields of sunflowers and wheat in springtime.

**Every season has its share of beauty**: crossing the countryside when the colors ignite—the red of the vines mixing with the silver of olive groves and the rusty tones of the woods—is an experience of its own. Art and history add the final touch.

## The gateway to the Val d'Orcia

Southeast of Siena lies the gateway to the Val d'Orcia: a narrow, compact entrance to one of the areas recognized by UNESCO in 2004 as a World Heritage Site. A landscape where nature and culture have long been intertwined in dynamic harmony—an outstanding example of human development integrated with the land, refined over generations into elegance and balance.

## San Quirico d'Orcia, where the loop begins

The journey begins in **San Quirico d'Orcia**, the ancient walled town perched on a hill separating the valleys of the Orcia. The Via Francigena passes straight through it. Two parallel streets can be crossed by bike in less than ten minutes, but the slow rhythm of cycle touring is needed to explore the many attractions within its well-preserved walls. Arriving from Siena, visitors are greeted by the grand Romanesque portal of the **Collegiate Church of Saints Quirico and Giulitta**, founded in 714, with its architrave relief of two monsters locked in battle. Following the Via Francigena through the village, you pass medieval buildings, workshops, and ancient taverns once used by pilgrims, now adapted for today's travelers.

Leaving your bike in a corner of Piazza Libertà, you can wander through the serene Italian-style Horti Leonini gardens, stroll to the Rose Garden, step inside the small church of Santa Maria Assunta, then return to the main square before exiting through the narrow, still-inhabited Porta dei Cappuccini.

Ahead stretches **the white ribbon of the Via Francigena.** From here the road climbs toward Montalcino, winding through vineyards and gravel lanes flanked by oak and holly woods. The final kilometers are a succession of curves opening onto vast horizons.

## Montalcino and the Abbey of Sant'Antimo

**Montalcino**, famed for its **Brunello wine**, is one of Italy's most beautiful hill towns. Its iconic fortress, built in 1361, was an innovative pentagonal stronghold, designed to withstand the new gunpowder weapons of the era. From its ramparts, the views are dizzying: to the south, the green of Mount Amiata; to the west, the horizon fading into the Tuscan Apennines; to the north, the outline of Siena. The town itself is a gem, with steep streets lined by architectural treasures: the Town Hall, the Bishop's Palace, and the churches of St. Augustine, St. Egidio, and St. Francis.

Ten kilometers further south lies the **Abbey of Sant'Antimo**, one of the most important Romanesque monuments in Tuscany. Set in the rolling countryside, it rises, solitary, majestic, and compact, an unforgettable sight.

## Castiglione d'Orcia and the road to Gallina

The route continues to **Castiglione d'Orcia**, with climbs and descents leading to the small cobbled square at its center. From the Aldobrandesca fortress, the view extends to the Rocca di Tentennano, a dramatic outcrop never conquered. Here it is worth braving the steep grades to visit this tiny hamlet, built around a cistern. At Via Santa Caterina No. 7 stands the house where Saint Catherine once stayed and, tradition says, learned to read and write. A short detour on the opposite side of Bagno Vignoni leads to the medieval **Spedaletto Castle**.

Despite the short distance, the day soon draws to a close. Riding toward Gallina, you find many options for lodging along this stretch of the Via Francigena. The climbs and descents demand focus; the gravel roads, emptier now in the cooler season, are almost silent. Only a few cyclists pass by, mostly foreigners, exchanging quick greetings before disappearing into the stillness of the hills, cypress-lined lanes, and woods—a silence hinting at the coming winter rest. In Gallina, a warm welcome awaits those on foot or on two wheels. It is time to rest.

## Day two: the road to Bagno Vignoni

It's time to rest. The next day there are miles to cover and sights to see. In the morning, the road leads toward Bagno Vignoni. The views widen, the landscape stretches out in gentle waves adorned with rows of cypress trees, and the air is crisp.

Bagno Vignoni, a medieval thermal village, was once a toll point for crossing the Orcia River. Hospitality here is centuries old: in the Middle Ages, the Amerighi family, acting on behalf of the Republic of Siena, collected tolls but were also required to host pilgrims on the **Via Francigena**, as recorded on a plaque beneath a carved lion's head. They built the Osteria del Leone, still today a fine stop for travelers.

It is difficult to spend only a few minutes in Bagno Vignoni. The tiny historic center is unique: its "piazza" is a large pool of steaming sulfurous water, fronted by the loggia of St. Catherine of Siena, who often came here. The waters, rising from a depth of one kilometer at about 40°C and surfacing at 30°C, have been renowned since Roman times. For cyclists—especially gravel riders—**the call of the wild is strong:** beyond the spas, ancient Roman pools and hidden basins among the vegetation invite a peaceful soak. In autumn, mist rising from the water filters the light through the trees, creating a magical atmosphere.

From here a short detour leads to Bagno Vignoni Alto, once considered a castle because of its tower, though without defensive walls. Behind the tower, Gregorian chants float from the church of San Biagio, where recordings are played daily, a balm for the soul. The church once housed a bronze crucifix by Giambologna, now kept in Montalcino. From the nearby gate, the view opens over the Val d'Orcia.

## Pienza, the ideal city

Continuing toward **Pienza**, the road alternates between the pale clay hills and the gray marl. Gradually, the landscape becomes almost lunar, shaped by moisture into pyramids of earth known as "biancane." Scattered farms, shifting microclimates, and irregular terrain after the first rains demand concentration. The silence is absolute, immersive.

Pienza, the "ideal city," soon appears. This fortified village became a papal residence in the mid-15th century, when Pope Pius II commissioned architect Bernardo Rossellino, a follower of Leon Battista Alberti, to redesign the town. Rossellino translated Alberti's humanist urban theories into reality, creating a city that embodied Renaissance ideals. Crossing it on foot with your bike in hand, you step into a concrete utopia—without the crowds of peak season. One of the great advantages of traveling off-season.

Here, a sandwich of Pienza's famous **pecorino cheese** makes the perfect break before wandering aimlessly through the streets, discovering splendid viewpoints: the panoramic terrace over the Val d'Orcia and Mount Amiata, or the Porta al Murello with its frescoed vault. Returning to Piazza Pio II, you can admire the spatial alignments of the cathedral and palaces: the trapezoidal square, the divergent angles, and the diagonal paving lines together create an amplified sense of perspective in the otherwise small space.

## Monticchiello, the Teatro Povero and a bike workshop

**Just a few minutes from town lies a small 7th-century church used in scenes of _Gladiator_.** From Pienza, the road leads toward Monticchiello, another fortified village, once a key defensive stronghold and frequent target of attacks. In 1967 it became famous for the *Teatro Povero di Monticchiello*: every summer for over 55 years, locals stage a collective play. The very first told the story of the resistance to Charles V's troops in the mid-16th century, connecting to the village's last battle, on April 6, 1944, when 70 partisans repelled an assault by 180 Fascist soldiers.

Exploring Monticchiello's steep streets, climbing to the Cassero Garden at the top, and returning to Piazza San Martino, home of the *Teatro Povero*, is an experience in itself. One of its projects links directly to cycling: a community-run workshop offering bike repairs while promoting eco-sustainable employment opportunities—a meeting place for visitors and residents alike, encouraging a slower, less consumerist form of tourism.

## Back to San Quirico by way of the Madonna di Vitaleta

Leaving the village, a final detour leads to the **Madonna di Vitaleta Chapel,** one of the most photographed icons of Tuscany, **accessible only on foot or by bike**. Framed by two rows of cypresses, the votive chapel was built where legend tells of a miraculous apparition centuries ago. Returning to the main road, the route passes pastures, farmhouses, and agriturismi, before circling back to San Quirico d'Orcia.

Few kilometers, a respectable amount of climbing, landscapes full of harmony. A leisurely journey, without haste, savoring the pleasure of wasting time—observing the countryside, enjoying views, good food, and local beauty.
