**Cycling through a timeless landscape** isn't just marketing —especially along the Via Francigena through the Crete Senesi. Compare today's photos with 14th- and 15th-century paintings by Pinturicchio, Sano di Pietro, the Lorenzetti brothers, and Simone Martini, and you'll find the same landscape: simple bridges, leafy clusters, erosion-carved hills, and solitary trees crowning lonely peaks. The stark contrast between Crete's muted earth tones and the intense blue sky remains unchanged.

Cyclists here face an endless succession of demanding hills, yet find reward in serene open spaces, profound silence, and **nature's ever-changing palette.**

## The farm at the end of the cypress road

The landscape is dotted not with cities but with medieval villages and isolated rural settlements that gaze at one another across the hills. On one of these hills stands a farm, accessible via a dirt road lined with iconic cypress trees. These trees blend so perfectly with the landscape that, as Elisabetta Gnudi Angelini says, **"no cyclist, traveler, or tourist can resist stopping for a photo."** Elisabetta, owner of the **Caparzo Estate** since 1998, presides over one of Montalcino's first *crus*—a vineyard producing wine uniquely tied to its location.

## An official stop on the Via Francigena

The Via Francigena passes directly through Caparzo, an official stop where travelers can have their ***Pilgrim Credential*** stamped. Thousands of pilgrims and cycling tourists visit annually—"some even with donkeys," Elisabetta adds with a smile. The clearly marked Pilgrim's Break offers local products and generous pours of wine.

## One of the historic Brunello producers

**Caparzo is one of the historic Brunello producers**, among the first 30 members of the Brunello di Montalcino Wine Consortium. The estate spans 200 hectares: 92 of vineyards (38 for Brunello), 4 of olive groves, 87 of woodland, and 20 of arable land and courtyards. Its diverse soils—clay, sand, sandstone, and schist—spread across twelve plots on all sides of Montalcino, ensuring consistent quality despite climate challenges.

## What the estate offers riders

**In 2024, Caparzo welcomed over 6,000 cycling tourists**—not counting participants in major events like *Strade Bianche*, *L'Eroica*, *Tuscany Trail*, or the *Walking Francigena Ultra Marathon*. "My son has ridden the Tuscany Trail three times, and the Giro d'Italia has passed through twice," says Elisabetta. **Both Caparzo and nearby Altesino offer bike repair stations with tools and water points—thoughtful amenities for those exploring local traditions by bike.**

## Staying, tasting, and sending things home

While bikepackers travel light, Caparzo's personalized shipping service lets guests send local products home to savor later. "It creates a virtuous cycle," Elisabetta notes. "Cyclists often return with family or friends." The estate features three farmhouse apartments amid the vineyards, offering **Val d'Orcia** views, wine tastings, and local cuisine—perfect for rest stops or longer stays.

The estate crafts traditional **Pici pasta** from stone-ground ancient wheat varieties (Verna and Senatore Cappelli) at nearby Spedaletto. Their offerings include Modena-aged **balsamic condiment**, local **honey**, neighboring farm **cheeses**, San Quirico **cured meats** from locally raised pigs, and fresh **olive oil** from their woodland Scopeto estate.

## Water, sun and an underground cellar

**Environmental respect** guides Caparzo's practices. "We collect rainwater in an artificial pond and were among the first to adopt landscape-friendly solar energy," explains Elisabetta. "Our underground cellar needs no heating or cooling." These sustainable choices align perfectly with low-impact travelers' values.

## When to ride this stretch

The stretch between Torrenieri and Montalcino captivates year-round: summer's sun-baked ochre earth, winter's white Crete wrapped in morning mist, spring's colorful blossoms, and autumn's warm red-orange palette.

This region's natural splendor, **Brunello excellence**, and Caparzo's thoughtful hospitality—paired with mild climate and cycling-friendly infrastructure—create an experience more epicurean than Dionysian. For here, happiness truly is "a pleasure of the soul," best savored while pedaling through hills with a glass of fine wine close at hand.
